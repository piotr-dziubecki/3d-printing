# Keep the sphere, fix the alignment

The 175 mm sphere with flush mics at ±90° is acoustically sound and needs no change to its size, ear positions, wall, mic pocket or seams. Its accuracy as an ITD/ILD/IACC instrument depends on three things outside the shell: aiming it (**1° of yaw moves ITD by 9–13 µs**), matching the two microphones (the IM73D122 is guaranteed only to **±1 dB**, so a pair can differ by 2 dB), and keeping the stand and chair out of the gated direct sound. Theory, the closest commercial precedents (Schoeps KFM 6 at 200 mm, KFM 360 at 180 mm, Neumann KFM 100, all with flush pressure capsules and no pinnae) and anthropometry all put 175 mm within a few millimetres of optimal and give no reason to move the ears. The geometry changes worth making are small and all sit on the slab: a level and yaw sight on top, a sturdier 3/8"-16 mount meant for a thin stem, and a shallower front notch. A 180° pan of the tripod swaps the two microphones exactly in space, because the ears are diametrically opposite on the horizontal circle. That gives a calibration that cancels channel mismatch without the roll-indexed mount one research note proposed. The fixture conforms to no dummy-head standard (ITU-T P.58, IEC 60318-7 and ANSI S3.36 all require a head and torso with pinnae), so its results are sphere-referenced and should be documented that way. Its value is as a clean, physically exact baseline for left/right comparisons. Most numbers below come from the research team's own Rayleigh-series computation (c = 343 m/s, a = 87.5 mm), which reproduces Duda and Martens' published ka = 1 at 624 Hz and the 3a/c low-frequency ITD limit. Where a claim is an inference rather than a measurement or a read source, the report says so.

## 175 mm and ±90° match both the precedents and the anthropometry

Spheres built as stand-ins for a head cluster at 180–200 mm, and every one of them uses flush pressure capsules without pinnae. Theile's sphere microphone, which became the Schoeps KFM 6, puts two pressure transducers flush on the sides of a 200 mm sphere and leaves out pinnae because dummy-head pinna cues "cause coloration problems" over loudspeakers ([Theile, JAES 1991](https://hauptmikrofon.de/theile/1991-2_Naturelness-of-stereo_JAES-1991.PDF); [B&H KFM 6](https://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/743597-REG/Schoeps_KFM_6_KFM6_Stereo_Sphere_Microphone.html)). Schoeps shrank the KFM 360 sphere to **180 mm** to widen its pickup angle from about 90° to 120°, so sphere size works as a tuning knob for the ITD-to-angle mapping ([micpedia](https://micpedia.com/?p=6062); [KFM 360 manual](https://www.manualsdir.com/manuals/593725/schoeps-kfm-360.html?page=3)). Measurement dummy heads belong to a different family: the Neumann KU 100 is 180 mm wide and 3.5 kg ([Neumann](https://www.neumann.com/en/products/microphones/ku-100/)), and HATS such as the HBK 5128 and GRAS KEMAR weigh 6.8–11.45 kg with pinnae, ear canals and ear simulators ([HBK 5128](https://www.bksv.com/-/media/literature/Product-Data/bp2573.ashx); [GRAS 45BB-6](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0755/9344/9805/files/GRAS_45BB-6.pdf)). No measurement product puts flush mics on a bare sphere, so this design is a hybrid: KFM geometry used as an instrument. The KFM 6 listed at USD 8,212 ([recordinghacks](https://recordinghacks.com/microphones/Schoeps/KFM-6)). Most HATS prices are not published.

Head-modelling research lands on the same radius. Duda and Martens call 8.75 cm the "traditional average head radius" ([Duda & Martens 1997](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/48m5k072)). Applying the Algazi, Avendano and Duda regression (r = 0.51·w½ + 0.019·h½ + 0.18·d½ + 32 mm, [AES 10188](https://www.aes.org/e-lib/browse.cfm?elib=10188), formula as reported by [Gamper et al.](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Anthropometric-parameterisation-Gamper.pdf)) to the CIPIC mean head dimensions ([CIPIC, WASPAA 2001](https://www.ece.ucdavis.edu/cipic/wp-content/uploads/sites/12/2015/04/cipic_WASSAP_2001_143.pdf)) gives **a ≈ 89 mm, a 178 mm sphere**, though that is the team's arithmetic on population means and not a value the paper reports. The move would raise ITD by 1.6%, about 4 µs for a 30° source. CIPIC head width varies by ±6.6% at one standard deviation, so the difference is smaller than the spread between listeners. Imaging diagnosis also rests on left-versus-right comparisons, which do not depend on radius to first order.

Moving the ears is not worth it either. Duda and Martens follow Blauert in placing ears at ±100° ([Duda & Martens 1997](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/48m5k072)), but the computed effect at a 30° source is small: ITD changes by under 5% and high-frequency ILD falls by 1–2.6 dB as the near ear loses part of its +6 dB pressure gain. CIPIC puts human ears **30 mm below the head centre and only 5 mm behind it** (SD 5.9 mm, so the rearward offset is not significant) ([CIPIC](https://www.ece.ucdavis.edu/cipic/wp-content/uploads/sites/12/2015/04/cipic_WASSAP_2001_143.pdf)). If an offset were ever wanted, a downward one would have better support than a rearward one. Computed by the Woodworth formula, a −20° ear elevation cuts horizontal-source ITD by about 6%, roughly what moving from 175 mm to 178 mm adds back. The ±90° placement has a practical benefit beyond matching the KFM convention: it makes the sphere symmetric under a 180° yaw, which the calibration section uses.

The missing torso is a stated limitation, not a defect. Early KEMAR work found torso cues of "a secondary nature" between 0.7 and 3.5 kHz, mainly as an elevation cue from shoulder reflection for sources above ear level ([Algazi et al., eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/7rf421w9)). For ear-height loudspeakers at ±30° the effect is close to mirror-symmetric left/right and largely cancels in difference measures. The standards rule out any claim of HATS equivalence. ANSI S3.36 and IEC TR 60959 share KEMAR-derived head, torso and pinna dimensions, ITU-T P.58 brackets the HEAD and B&K shapes, and all require a torso ([ISVR report 6824](https://generic.wordpress.soton.ac.uk/isvr-new/wp-content/uploads/sites/422/2022/07/6824-R01.pdf)). IEC TS 60318-7 asks designs used outside its scope to document their variations ([IEC TS 60318-7 preview](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/17312/24b8f93023b7409ca72e26ddd126e437/IEC-TS-60318-7-2011.pdf)). The honest label is "spherical-head binaural probe", with the IEC coordinate convention (reference point midway between the ears, 0° front, +90° right ear).

## A 30° speaker should read 386 µs low and 267 µs high

Because the sphere is a rigid body with a closed-form solution, the fixture can be checked against exact numbers. At 2–4 m the normalised range r/a is 23–46, well above the ρ < 5 threshold where Duda and Martens find range effects begin, so the plane-wave solution applies ([Duda & Martens 1997](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/48m5k072)). For a source at 30° and ears at ±90°, the computed interaural phase delay is about **386 µs below 300 Hz** (the Kuhn limit 3a/c·sin φ = 383 µs) and settles at **267–280 µs above 3 kHz**, close to the Woodworth value of 261 µs. Duda and Martens confirm the "50% greater" low-frequency delay and the Woodworth match for the high-frequency limit ([Duda & Martens 1997](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/48m5k072)). The transition runs from about 500 Hz to 1.5 kHz around ka = 1 at 624 Hz, and group delay there swings between about 180 and 420 µs. Any single-number ITD from that band is unreliable.

| Source at 30°, ears ±90° | 100 Hz | 500 Hz | 1 kHz | 2 kHz | 4 kHz | 8 kHz | 20 kHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ITD, phase delay (µs) | 386 | 399 | 320 | 289 | 279 | 272 | 267 |
| ILD (dB) | 0.0 | 1.5 | 4.9 | 4.4 | 5.9 | 7.9 | 10.5 |

The table is the fixture's acceptance target. The ILD rises smoothly with only a small dip near 1.5–2 kHz, and that smoothness makes the sphere a better diagnostic baseline than a pinna head: a deviation measured in a room comes from the room or speakers, not from concha notches. The flip side is that sphere ILDs underestimate real-head ILDs above about 4 kHz, and a 2026 comparison of measurement systems makes the same point ([Acta Acustica aacus250090](https://acta-acustica.edpsciences.org/articles/aacus/abs/2026/01/aacus250090/aacus250090.html), abstract only). The same paper puts the perceptual weight of ITD below about 1.5 kHz and of ILD above about 2.5 kHz, which tells the analysis where to look for each cue.

Two pieces of sphere physics will show up in real rooms and should not be mistaken for faults. A side-wall reflection arriving from near ±90° lands the far microphone on the contralateral "bright spot", where two creeping waves meet and produce strong ripples. Incidence angles of 150–170° show two distinct pulses ([Duda & Martens 1997](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/48m5k072)). The direct sound from ±30° speakers reaches the far ear at 120° incidence and stays clear of that zone. Second, in a diffuse field the computed coherence between antipodal points on the sphere falls to zero at about **600 Hz, against about 980 Hz for two free-field mics 175 mm apart**, so the sphere decorrelates like a head, not like a spaced pair. Bradley found that a B&K HATS's IACC matched an omni pair about twice its physical ear spacing ([Bradley 1994](https://www.akutek.info/Mitt%20Bibliotek/BRADLEY_Comparison%20of%20concert%20hall%20measurements%20of%20spatial%20impression_nrcc37817.pdf)). The team found no published comparison of sphere IACC with dummy-head IACC, so absolute sphere IACC values are not interchangeable with published hall data measured on a HATS.

The fixture's own validation is therefore the main evidence of its accuracy. The Duda and Martens bowling ball, a 10.9 cm-radius sphere with a probe mic on a 1.3 cm rod, matched theory "remarkably" well ([Duda & Martens 1997](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/48m5k072)). It is the template: rotate this sphere against one speaker in 5° steps, gate the direct sound, compare with the series, and keep the resulting curve as the sphere's own ITD/ILD-to-angle map.

## The shell, port and seams pass; small hidden cavities are the risk

At the microphone, the design already does what the application notes ask. The carrier PCB face forms the outer surface, so the port is a 1.6 mm neck straight into the MEMS. The IM73D122 datasheet recommends a PCB port of 0.8 mm diameter, larger than the package port. The bare part already rises **+3 dB by 19 kHz and peaks at 37 kHz** ([Infineon IM73D122 datasheet](https://www.infineon.com/assets/row/public/documents/24/49/infineon-im73d122-datasheet-en.pdf)). InvenSense AN-1003 says hole size does not matter above 0.25 mm and warns that a wide gasket or cavity between board and case forms a Helmholtz resonator with a high-frequency peak ([AN-1003](https://www.cdiweb.com/datasheets/invensense/Mounting-Recommendations.pdf)). The team's lumped estimate shows why the hazard is a hidden cavity, not the neck. A 0.8 mm, 1.6 mm port in front of 1 mm³ resonates near 26 kHz, but 5 mm³ (a solder-mask relief ring, an underfilled pad gap) pulls it down to about 11.5 kHz, inside the ILD band. The package front volume is not in the datasheet, so these numbers locate the risk rather than predict a peak.

Flushness is not a concern at the current tolerance. A flat 15 mm disc on an 87.5 mm sphere has a sagitta of 0.32 mm, which is why the README puts the centre ≤0.35 mm below the sphere when the rim is flush. That recess is λ/57 at 20 kHz, with a quarter-wave depth resonance near 286 kHz. Being open and wide, it is a negligible scatterer, not a resonator. The model's existing assertion that the carrier sits less than 0.5 mm below the pole is the right guard. The seal around the carrier matters more than the step, because a leak from the conduit into the port's front path would put the internal volume in series with the microphone. The 0.2 mm layer terracing is λ/86 at 20 kHz and acoustically irrelevant. The hairline seams sit at 76.8° from each ear and are negligible unless they open into a lip, which would mostly disturb the contralateral high-frequency level.

The shell counts as rigid. PLA at 4.5 mm gives about 5.6 kg/m², far above air impedance, and the team's breathing-mode estimate is **about 4–5 kHz** (using a PLA modulus of 2–3.5 GPa from [PMC6981541](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6981541)). Sound excites that mode only weakly, and the sand further loads and damps it. MEMS membranes are insensitive to vibration, at about **40 dB SPL-equivalent at 1 m/s²** against 57 dB for an electret ([AN-1003](https://www.cdiweb.com/datasheets/invensense/Mounting-Recommendations.pdf)). Footstep-level vibration therefore stays below the mic's roughly 21 dB(A) noise floor, derived from its 73 dB(A) SNR. Of what reaches the recording, shell radiation and rocking on the stand dominate, not inertial pickup. A 3D-printed head with lavalier mics matched KEMAR's ILDs at 90° and differed in ITD by **62.5 µs at 90°** ([Lu et al., arXiv 2505.04548](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.04548)), which shows that geometry, not print surface, sets ITD accuracy. Each millimetre of ear-point error is worth up to 2.9 µs.

The remaining surface features are all on the median plane, 90° from both ears, so they act almost identically on both channels. The front notch is a 1.5 mm V-groove lying along the horizontal great circle, in the lit path of both speakers, and is λ/11 at 20 kHz. That is larger than any other departure in the forward hemisphere, though it is still symmetric. The OLED glass sits at r = 82.5 mm, up to 5 mm below the sphere surface, in a window of about 24 × 17 mm on the back. The USB slot and the bay behind it form a Helmholtz resonator the README estimates at about 200 Hz. At 200 Hz ka is 0.32, so the vent acts as a weak point source at the back that feeds both ears nearly in phase. It affects level slightly and ILD or ITD hardly at all, and fibre in the bay lowers its Q at no cost. The code places the OLED window centre about 6 mm and the USB slot about 13 mm toward the right cap, a slight left/right asymmetry. At the back and at these sizes it is a second-order effect and does not justify a slab redesign. One open-tape test settles the vent question empirically.

## Errors come from aiming, channel matching and the stand

The geometry error budget is dominated by placement. On this sphere the ITD slope near the midline is 2a/c to 3a/c, **8.9 µs per degree at high frequency and 13.4 µs per degree at low frequency**. A 10 µs ITD target therefore needs yaw within about ±1°, ten times tighter than the ±10° that Witew, Lindau and colleagues found acceptable for IACC with a FABIAN HATS ([Witew et al., DAGA 2010](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/DAGA_2010/data/articles/000468.pdf)). At 3 m, a laser on the front meridian has to land within ±26 mm of the speaker midpoint for ±0.5°. Lateral seat offset is worse: **1 cm sideways changes the left/right path difference by about 29 µs**. That shift is a real imaging effect and needs a centring reference so it is not confused with yaw, either a floor mark or matched tape distances to both tweeters within about 3 mm. Pitch hardly matters (5° costs about 1 µs), but roll lifts one ear, so the ear axis needs a level. The fixture as designed has no level and no yaw reference beyond the front notch.

Microphone matching is the second budget. The IM73D122 is factory-trimmed to −26 ±1 dBFS, with **SNR 73 dB(A) at a 3.072 MHz clock**, 69 dB(A) in the 768 kHz mode with only 8 kHz bandwidth, **AOP 122 dB SPL**, LFRO 20 Hz, and only typical phase and group-delay figures, such as 60 µs group delay at 250 Hz ([Infineon datasheet](https://www.infineon.com/assets/row/public/documents/24/49/infineon-im73d122-datasheet-en.pdf)). With no unit-to-unit phase specification, low-frequency ITD is the weak point. A 2 Hz spread in the LFRO corner would show up as roughly 50 µs of false ITD at 75 Hz, though only about 1 µs at 500 Hz. This is a first-order model, not a measured pair. The shared PDM clock fixes the inter-channel timing; the opposite-edge offset of about 163 ns is constant and negligible. Playback on another clock stretches both channels equally and leaves ITD and ILD untouched.

The research notes proposed a roll-180° mount to swap the mics. The ±90° geometry offers a simpler swap. Rotating the sphere 180° about the vertical axis through its centre, which is where the tripod nut already sits, puts each microphone exactly where the other one was, in any sound field and from any source, as long as the 180° is accurate. With run A = τ + m and run B = −τ + m, the acoustic difference is τ = (A − B)/2 and the channel mismatch is m = (A + B)/2, per band, for both ITD and ILD. A 1° error in the rotation displaces the mic by 1.5 mm, a couple of microseconds. Only the median-plane features break the symmetry, which is a further reason to keep the front notch shallow. This method is the report's own derivation from the geometry; no source describes it. It removes channel mismatch but not yaw error, which still needs the sight line. The swap needs only a pan head with a 180° scale, plus a sight usable in both directions.

The stand and the room set the clean window. The Duda and Martens rod (1.3 cm) and the ISO 3382 rule of at least a quarter wavelength from reflecting surfaces, with receivers **1.2 m above the floor** ([ISO 3382:1997 sample](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/2354/da1625f2a0c9494ab0ac112528ff5850/ISO-3382-1997.pdf)), both point to a thin stem. A rod at most 12–16 mm across and at least 15–20 cm long moves the tripod-head reflection beyond about 1 ms. Toledo and Møller could not exclude setup reflections from their KU 100 filters without shortening them ([Toledo & Møller, AES 2009](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/227876843/2009_Toledo_and_M_ller_AES_Convention.pdf)). A chair backrest 10–20 cm behind the sphere adds a symmetric echo at 0.6–1.2 ms, inside the 80 ms early-IACC window. Mount threads on measurement heads are 3/8"-16 (HBK 5128-B, HEAD HMS V) or 5/8"-27 (KU 100); none of those read uses 1/4"-20 ([HMS V datasheet](https://documents.thermofisher.com/TFS-Assets/ANZ/Datasheets/head-acoustics-hms-v-artificial-head-1502.pdf); [HBK 5128](https://www.bksv.com/-/media/literature/Product-Data/bp2573.ashx)). At 2.4 kg on a nut that a 4.5 mm camera screw engages for about two turns, the current mount is the mechanically weakest point of the design.

The analysis practice is settled enough to adopt directly. ISO 3382-1 defines IACC as the maximum of the normalised interaural cross-correlation within ±1 ms, with an early 0–80 ms window and a late window from 80 ms. It does not specify band averaging, so IACC_E3 (mean of 500 Hz, 1 kHz and 2 kHz) is the common choice ([Girón et al. 2012](https://journals.pan.pl/Content/101627/PDF/14_paper.pdf)). The quoted JND is 0.05 or 0.075 depending on the source ([Bradley, ISRA 2010](https://www.acoustics.asn.au/conference_proceedings/ICA2010/cdrom-ISRA2010/Papers/O3a.pdf)). The sphere's maximum ITD of 0.66–0.77 ms fits inside the lag window. IACC fluctuates strongly with small position changes ([de Vries et al., AES 9191](https://www.aes.org/e-lib/browse.cfm?elib=9191)), so averaging three to five positions a few centimetres apart is advisable. Exponential sweeps separate distortion and tolerate clock mismatch ([Farina 2007](https://angelofarina.it/Public/papers/238-NordicSound2007.pdf)). Katz and Noisternig found ITD estimators "highly variable" on the same data, with onset-threshold and cross-correlation methods on low-passed responses the more reliable ([HAL 01107079](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01107079), abstract only). REW computes early, late and full IACC per octave from a pair of measurements but gives no ITD or ILD output ([REW help](https://www.roomeqwizard.com/betahelp/help/html/iacc.html)), so a small Python analysis script has to fill that gap.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. The sphere was designed around acoustic purity at the ears, and that part holds up: none of the shell, port, seam or surface details threatens accuracy at the scale of the wavelengths involved. The uncertainty that matters is metrological. Yaw, centring, a ±1 dB pair tolerance with unspecified phase, and setup reflections each outweigh every geometric detail of the shell. The geometry changes that pay off help the user aim, level and mount the head. The largest single improvement is free: because the ears are antipodal on the horizontal circle, a 180° pan swaps them exactly, so the existing ±90° layout already contains its own channel calibration.

The remaining unknowns are empirical, and the fixture can settle them itself: the package front volume behind the port, the real pair mismatch below 300 Hz, the bay vent's effect, and how sphere IACC relates to HATS IACC. A validation sweep in 5° steps against the Rayleigh-series table answers the first three and turns the device from a plausible design into a characterised instrument. The fourth needs a side-by-side measurement with a dummy head, which no published study has done.
